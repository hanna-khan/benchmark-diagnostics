Primum Non Nocere

First, do no harm, and the pathologist’s silent influence

  • Ethics
  • 3 min read
  • 2026
  • Benchmark Diagnostics

In this article

  1. The oath in an age of intervention
  2. How does the oath apply to the pathologist?
  3. Between expectation and limitation
  4. A noble tradition
Download PDF

Key takeaways

  • A pathology report steers treatment, so errors or poor wording can cause overtreatment, undertreatment or distress.
  • Resisting premature certainty can itself be an act of ethical medicine.
  • Honest communication with the clinician is often the most responsible course.

01The oath in an age of intervention

In this era of unprecedented technological advancement, the ancient Hippocratic principle, primum non nocere (“first, do no harm”), has become increasingly difficult to uphold in its purest form.

Modern medicine is built upon interventions: surgeries, medications, devices, and procedures that often carry significant risks and side effects. If treatment were entirely harmless, there would be no need for consent forms, warnings, or lengthy discussions of complications. The long list of iatrogenic disorders stands as testimony to this reality.

02How does the oath apply to the pathologist?

At first glance, the pathologist appears removed from direct patient harm. Unlike surgeons or clinicians, we do not prescribe medications, perform operations, or administer therapies. Yet few physicians wield such silent influence over patient care.

A clinician’s treatment decisions often rest heavily upon the pathology report. A diagnostic error, an overcalled malignancy, a missed lesion, or even a poorly worded report may lead to overtreatment, undertreatment, emotional distress, or irreversible harm.

Without ever touching the patient, the pathologist may still become a direct cause of iatrogenesis.

For this reason, the pathologist must guard the principle with exceptional vigilance. Unlike many therapeutic specialties where some harm may be unavoidable in pursuit of a cure, pathology offers a unique opportunity to remain faithful to the oath through diligence, restraint, honesty, and intellectual humility.

03Between expectation and limitation

The pressure to render a definitive diagnosis can weigh heavily, particularly with imperfect material or incomplete information. Such limitations are woven into everyday practice: inadequate clinical history, nonrepresentative biopsies, poor tissue preservation, technical artifacts, and conflicting morphologic findings.

Practice pointResisting the temptation to provide premature certainty is itself an act of ethical medicine. When the grounds for a confident diagnosis are absent, caution becomes wisdom rather than weakness.

Honest communication with the treating clinician, whether through a carefully crafted report or a direct conversation, is often the most responsible course of action and ultimately the one that best serves the patient.

04A noble tradition

The reverence historically granted to the medical profession is unlike that afforded to almost any other calling: the village physician in a nineteenth-century countryside, or the starship doctor tending an injured Klingon. Equally inspiring are the real figures whose observations transformed medicine: the nurse who recognized the significance of a periumbilical nodule, or the pathologist whose discovery helped reveal the bacterial cause of peptic ulcer disease.

This tradition, from Imhotep to Hippocrates, from Ibn Sina to Pasteur and Fleming, was built upon an uncompromising dedication to alleviating human suffering through truth, discipline, and service.

Perhaps the modern physician cannot always avoid causing harm. But we can remain honest about our limitations, humble in the face of uncertainty, and steadfast in our commitment to minimizing suffering whenever possible.
