Artifacts in Pathology

Recognizing diagnostic pitfalls from specimen to slide

  • 4 min read
  • 2026
  • Arif Usmani, M.D.

In this article

  2. Artifacts in the physician’s office

  5. Conclusion
Download PDF

Key takeaways

  • An artifact is any alteration that interferes with accurate interpretation beyond the disease itself.
  • They arise in three stages: physician’s office, transportation and the laboratory.
  • Tangential sectioning, crush, cautery and freezing are classic ways to be fooled.

01What is an artifact?

Unlike cultural or historical artifacts, pathology slides do not carry archaeological significance. Yet, much like a historian interpreting an ancient object, the pathologist must determine the artifact’s origin, mechanism, and implications.

In anatomic pathology, an artifact is any alteration that interferes with the accurate interpretation of the specimen beyond the inherent complexity of the disease process itself. Artifacts may range from tissue desiccation to extraneous tissue fragments, commonly called “floaters.”

From the moment a biopsy is obtained until the final slide reaches the microscope, numerous steps are involved, and artifacts may arise at any stage. These can compromise diagnosis and, in serious cases, place patient care and the pathologist’s professional liability at risk.

  1. Physician’s officeAnesthesia, instruments, cautery, bleeding
  2. TransportationFreezing and fixation
  3. LaboratoryGrossing to coverslip

02Artifacts in the physician’s office

During anesthesia

Local anesthetic injected directly into a lesion may displace tissue and distort the normal microscopic architecture.

Crush and forceps marks

Tissue grasped too firmly, or with dull instruments, shows crush artifact and “teeth marks”. A severe crush can render a biopsy nearly uninterpretable.

Fulguration (cautery)

Electrocautery or a heated blade causes thermal injury. Epithelial cells may turn spindle-shaped with loss of nuclear detail, while collagen and elastic fibers coagulate into dense clumps separated by empty spaces.

Hemorrhage

Excessive bleeding into the specimen may obscure findings or mimic pathologic hemorrhage seen in vascular disease or coagulation disorders.

03Artifacts during transportation

Freezing artifact

Ten percent formalin freezes below about 12°F (−11°C). Ice crystals give tissue a characteristic “Swiss cheese” look, so overnight outdoor drop boxes in winter should be avoided whenever possible.

Fixation artifact

If tissue is not fully submerged in fixative, partial drying and autolysis can cause loss of cellular detail and poor preservation.

04Artifacts in the laboratory

Grossing station: floaters

Tissue fragments that do not belong to the patient may be carried over by inadequately cleaned forceps, blades or work surfaces.

Tissue processor

Tiny fragments can transfer between cassettes. Improper protocols, malfunction, exhausted reagents or inadequate fixation add further artifacts.

Embedding: tangential sectioning

If tissue shifts before the paraffin sets, it may be cut tangentially, which can exaggerate cytologic atypia and look falsely alarming. Improper embedding may also lose the epidermis or surface entirely.

Microtomy

Blade sharpness and blade-to-block angle matter: variable thickness, ribbon curling, chatter, wrinkling or fragmentation. A dull or nicked blade leaves streaks, tears and linear defects.

Tissue bath

Fragments left in the flotation bath from a previous specimen may adhere to later slides.

Staining

Aged or improperly prepared stains can precipitate on the slide. Acidic formaldehyde reacting with hemoglobin forms acid formaldehyde hematin pigment.

Coverslipping

Air bubbles trapped under the coverslip can partially obscure the tissue section.

05Conclusion

Artifacts are more than technical imperfections; they are potential diagnostic pitfalls.

Recognizing their origin and appearance is essential for accurate interpretation and patient safety. A skilled pathologist must not only diagnose disease but also distinguish true pathology from changes introduced during specimen collection, handling, processing, and slide preparation.
